\section{Error analysis}\label{sec:error}

This section asks where the largest errors occur (which transistor case, gate, input
combination and corner conditions) and why. All tables and figures are generated by
\texttt{report/make\_analysis.py} from the committed results. Transistor errors are measured on the
\emph{validation} rows, so the test set stays untouched.

\subsection{Transistor models}\label{sec:errtrans}
% Based on text by Joanna (P2).
Table~\ref{tab:transvds} breaks the 95th-percentile error of every current down by $|V_{DS}|$ and
Table~\ref{tab:transtemp} by temperature.

\paragraph{ON-state $I_D$, $I_S$ near $V_D = V_S$.}
The largest relative errors are the ON-state drain and source currents at small $|V_{DS}|$. For
\texttt{nmos\_on} the p95 error of $I_D$ is \TrVdsLowNmosOnId{} below \qty{1}{\milli\volt},
against \TrVdsHighNmosOnId{} above \qty{0.3}{\volt} (\texttt{pmos\_on}: \TrVdsLowPmosOnId{}
against \TrVdsHighPmosOnId). The channel current passes through zero at $V_D = V_S$, so a small
absolute error is a large relative one, and because \LowVdsFrac{} of the ON rows were placed near
that point on purpose (Section~\ref{sec:oversampling}), these rows dominate the ON statistics of
Tables~\ref{tab:models} and~\ref{tab:chosen}. They matter little for leakage: inside a gate the
channel current of such a transistor is solved from KCL (Section~\ref{sec:kcl}). The ON currents
that do enter the gate leakage stay accurate at small $|V_{DS}|$: the gate current has a p95 of
\TrVdsLowNmosOnIg{} (\texttt{nmos\_on}) and \TrVdsLowPmosOnIg{} (\texttt{pmos\_on}), the body
current \TrVdsLowNmosOnIb{} and \TrVdsLowPmosOnIb{}. The ON body current becomes harder at large
$|V_{DS}|$ (\TrVdsHighNmosOnIb{} above \qty{0.3}{\volt}), where impact ionisation and
gate-induced drain leakage add a body current that grows steeply with the drain field.

\paragraph{OFF-state source current.}
OFF-state $I_S$ is tiny and changes sign near $V_D = V_S$, where the junction leakage at the source
balances the channel current: \BelowFloorNmosOffIs{} (\texttt{nmos\_off}) and
\BelowFloorPmosOffIs{} (\texttt{pmos\_off}) of the validation rows are below the \IFloor{}
floor. It is the hardest OFF current at every bias (Table~\ref{tab:transvds}). Deriving $I_S$ from
Kirchhoff's law (Section~\ref{sec:models}) helps, but cannot remove the error at small currents.
It improves strongly with temperature (\texttt{nmos\_off}: \TrTempColdNmosOffIs{} below
\qty{0}{\degreeCelsius}, \TrTempHotNmosOffIs{} above \qty{80}{\degreeCelsius}), because the
subthreshold channel current, and with it $I_S$, grows exponentially with $T$ and moves away from
the floor. The OFF-state gate current lies entirely below the floor (around $10^{-22}$\,A) and is
irrelevant for leakage.

\paragraph{Junction (body) current and temperature.}
The OFF-state body current shows the opposite trend: for \texttt{nmos\_off} its p95 grows from
\TrTempColdNmosOffIb{} below \qty{0}{\degreeCelsius} to \TrTempHotNmosOffIb{} above
\qty{80}{\degreeCelsius} (\texttt{pmos\_off}: \TrTempColdPmosOffIb{} to \TrTempHotPmosOffIb).
At low temperature the junction current sits on the constant floor visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:trends} and is trivial to predict; at high temperature the thermally generated
junction current rises steeply and the model has to follow it.

\paragraph{Coverage of the operating point.}
The decisive finding of the transistor-level analysis was that uniform sampling barely covers the
bias points a transistor sees inside a static gate: an ON transistor sits at $V_{DS}\approx 0$,
an OFF transistor has its source on its own rail and its drain on the other. The rules of
Section~\ref{sec:oversampling} were added for this reason; the gate simulations confirmed the
problem but were never used to train, tune or select a model. Table~\ref{tab:history} shows the
effect at gate level.

\subsection{Gates}\label{sec:errgates}

\paragraph{What the SPICE reference shows.}
The gate SPICE results contain several checks and one surprise. NAND2~\texttt{01} and
INV~\texttt{0} have the same leakage at every corner (largest deviation \NandZeroOneInvDev): with
$b = 1$ the lower NMOS ties $x$ to ground, so the gate is electrically an inverter with input~0
plus an OFF PMOS at $V_{DS}\approx 0$ that adds nothing. This is a direct wiring check of the
decks. NOR2 shows the classic stacking effect: with both inputs high, two OFF PMOS in series cut the
leakage to \NorStackARatio{} of NOR2~\texttt{01} (median; lower in \NorStackALower{} corners) and
\NorStackBRatio{} of NOR2~\texttt{10}. The intermediate node settles at \VyOneOneFrac{}\,$V_{DD}$,
which reverse-biases the gate of the upper PMOS and lowers its $V_{DS}$.

NAND2~\texttt{00}, however, leaks \emph{more} than NAND2~\texttt{01} (median ratio
\NandZeroARatio; lower in only \NandZeroALower{} corners) and NAND2~\texttt{10}
(\NandZeroBRatio). Its NMOS side does benefit from stacking, but both PMOS are now ON with their
gate at~0 and their source at $V_{DD}$, so there are two gate-tunnelling paths from VDD instead of
one. On this low-power card the threshold voltage is high and the subthreshold current small, so
the tunnelling current is comparable to it, and the extra tunnelling path outweighs the stacking
saving. Table~\ref{tab:nandtemp} confirms the mechanism: subthreshold current grows steeply with
temperature and tunnelling hardly at all, so at high temperature the stacking effect returns.

The internal node voltages follow the same physics. In NAND2~\texttt{00} node $x$ floats up to
\VxZeroZeroMin--\VxZeroZeroMax{} (about \VxZeroZeroFrac{}\,$V_{DD}$), until the two OFF NMOS carry
the same current; this small positive voltage is what makes the upper NMOS ``more off''. In
NAND2~\texttt{10} the ON upper NMOS can only pass a weak~1 and $x$ stops at about
\VxOneZeroFrac{}\,$V_{DD}$, a threshold below the supply; in NOR2~\texttt{10} the ON lower PMOS
passes a weak~0 and $y$ stops at about \VyOneZeroFrac{}\,$V_{DD}$. A node tied to a rail through ON
transistors stays within \VxOnRailMax{} of it, and $n_1$ in AND2/OR2 within \NOneRailMax.

Leakage rises with temperature in every case, but by very different factors
(Table~\ref{tab:temptrend}, the \TempGroupSize{} hottest corners, \TempHotMin--\TempHotMax, against
the \TempGroupSize{} coldest, \TempColdMin--\TempColdMax). The strong-rise cases (factor
\StrongHotColdMin--\StrongHotColdMax: \StrongCases) are exactly those with every PMOS OFF, whose
leakage is pure subthreshold conduction. Every other case has at least one ON PMOS whose gate
tunnelling makes up a large, nearly temperature-independent part of the leakage, and rises only
by a factor \WeakHotColdMin--\WeakHotColdMax.

\paragraph{Gate error against the corner conditions.}
Figure~\ref{fig:errcorner} and Tables~\ref{tab:errtemp} and~\ref{tab:errvth} relate the gate error
to the corner. The error falls with temperature: the median error is \GateColdMedianErr{} below
\qty{0}{\degreeCelsius} and \GateHotMedianErr{} above \qty{80}{\degreeCelsius}, with the bias
dropping from \GateColdBias{} to \GateHotBias. Of all corner parameters the signed error correlates
most with \GateDriverFirst{} (Spearman $\rho = \GateDriverFirstRho$), followed by
\GateDriverSecond{} ($\rho = \GateDriverSecondRho$) and \GateDriverThird{}
($\rho = \GateDriverThirdRho$). These point to one mechanism, which the SPICE oracle confirms
(Table~\ref{tab:errorsources}): the largest single error source is \ErrorSourceFirst{}, the gate
tunnelling current of the ON PMOS. It depends exponentially on the PMOS oxide thickness, and its
share of the gate leakage is largest when subthreshold conduction is weak, i.e.\ at low temperature
and high $|V_{th0}|$. At high temperature the leakage is dominated by OFF transistors at the
pinned full-$V_{DD}$ operating point, which the models predict best. The NMOS threshold has no
clear effect.

\paragraph{Worst cases.}
The worst gate cases and their causes are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:gates}: NAND2~\texttt{10}
suffers from the ML drain current of the upper NMOS, which is ON at a substantial $|V_{DS}|$ with
its source floating at $x$; NOR2~\texttt{10} and~\texttt{11} from the OFF PMOS source current.
The worst rows (Table~\ref{tab:gateworst}) are mostly NAND2~\texttt{10} and AND2~\texttt{10},
whose first stage is the same NAND2, plus a few NOR2 rows; they occur over the whole temperature
range, so they are tied to the input state rather than to a corner.

\paragraph{Gate-voltage snap and extrapolation.}
Both possible artefacts of the composition are harmless. The snap of the AND2/OR2 inverter gate to
the nearest rail is at most \GateSnapMax{}. In \ExtrapRows{} gate rows (\ExtrapCorners{} corners,
all at the highest supply voltages) a transistor input lies slightly outside the range it was
trained on: \ExtrapWhat{}, by at most \ExtrapMaxOver{} (\ExtrapMaxOverPct{} of the range). These
are ON PMOS with both drain and source at $V_{DD}$, slightly above the largest drain voltage
drawn in Part~A, and their channel current is replaced by KCL anyway. Those rows are no worse
than the rest: median error \ExtrapMedianErr{} against \NoExtrapMedianErr, maximum
\ExtrapMaxErr.

\paragraph{SPICE numerical floor.}
The reference itself has a resolution limit. The Kirchhoff residual of the single-PMOS decks
stays around $10^{-16}$\,A (Table~\ref{tab:kirchhoff}), while the smallest gate leakage is
\GateSpiceMin{} (\GateSpiceMinCase). Relative to that, the numerical floor is
\SpiceFloorRel, so for the smallest rows the SPICE reference is only good to about one or two per
cent. This does not affect the larger leakages that dominate the statistics.

\paragraph{The \texttt{rgatemod} convergence problem.}
With the original card (\texttt{rgatemod=1}), an earlier gate run had 15 of the 3\,600 gate decks, all
AND2/OR2 with output~0, that did not converge cleanly. Some were rejected although ngspice
had recovered with its own fallback; others fell back to a transient operating point with
unreliable values, including one negative supply current; one failed outright. The internal
gate-resistance node that \texttt{rgatemod=1} adds carries essentially no current at DC.
Setting \texttt{rgatemod=0} (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) made every deck converge and changed the
leakage of the decks that had converged before by about $4\times10^{-6}$ (median) and
$9\times10^{-5}$ (p95), relative. The Part~A data were regenerated with the same setting, so the
whole project uses one card.

\subsection{Test circuit}\label{sec:errcircuit}

\paragraph{Composition versus model error.}
To separate the error of the hierarchical composition from that of the ML models, the circuit was
also composed from the Part~B \emph{SPICE} gate results (gate $I_{VDD}$ and $I_{in}$ in place of
the ML values) with the same loading term. This composition reproduces the circuit SPICE leakage
with a mean accuracy of \CompSpiceMeanAcc{} (median error \CompSpiceMedianErr, maximum
\CompSpiceMaxErr). The loading approximation (ideal $0$/$V_{DD}$ gate inputs, $I_{in}$ split evenly
over a gate's high pins) is therefore essentially exact for this circuit, while leaving the
loading out gives a bias of \CompSpiceNoLoadBias. Measured against this SPICE-based composition, the
ML prediction has a mean accuracy of \CompMlMeanAcc, practically the final circuit accuracy: the
whole circuit error is inherited from the gate-level ML error.

\paragraph{Bias accumulation.}
The circuit leakage is a sum over \CircuitNumGates{} gates plus the loading term, so the part of
the gate error that is common to all instances of a corner adds up instead of averaging out
(Section~\ref{sec:circuit}). This is visible in the temperature dependence
(Table~\ref{tab:errtemp}): the circuit bias is \CircuitColdBias{} below \qty{0}{\degreeCelsius}
and \CircuitHotBias{} above \qty{80}{\degreeCelsius}, and the median error falls from
\CircuitColdMedianErr{} to \CircuitHotMedianErr. At low temperature the largely systematic PMOS tunnelling
error dominates; at high temperature the remaining
gate errors have mixed signs and largely cancel. Hence the signed circuit error correlates with
temperature (Spearman $\rho = \CircuitTempRho$) much more clearly than with the input vector,
whose influence is small (Table~\ref{tab:circuit}).
