\section{Transistor models}\label{sec:models}

For each of the four cases one model predicts all four terminal currents ($I_D$, $I_G$, $I_S$,
$I_B$) from the raw inputs of Table~\ref{tab:ranges}. Each model is saved together with its
feature code and target transform, so later stages call it on raw voltages and get currents in
amperes back.

\subsection{Input features}
Within one case $V_G$ and $V_B$ are always $0$ or $V_{DD}$, so they carry no information on
their own; the terminal voltage \emph{differences} do. From the raw inputs we build
\NumFeatures{} features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item supply and temperature: $V_{DD}$, $T$ (K), $1/T$, thermal voltage $v_T = kT/q$;
  \item geometry: $W$, $\ln W$;
  \item bias: $V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$, $V_{BS}$, $V_{GD}$, $V_{BD}$, $|V_{DS}|$;
  \item subthreshold physics: $V_{GS}/v_T$, $V_{DS}/v_T$, $(V_{GS}-|V_{th0}|)/v_T$ and the drain
        factor $1-e^{-|V_{DS}|/v_T}$;
  \item process: the five parameters divided by their nominal values from the model card (so
        they lie in a narrow band around 1), plus $1/t_{ox}$, since gate tunnelling grows
        steeply as the oxide gets thinner.
\end{itemize}
The physics features put the dominant exponential dependences (subthreshold conduction, its
temperature dependence, tunnelling) into a form in which the log-current is close to linear, which
every model type benefits from. For PMOS every voltage difference is negated ($V_{SG}$ instead of
$V_{GS}$) and $|V_{th0}|$ is used, so PMOS features read like NMOS ones: a larger $V_{GS}$ always
means ``more on''. The same function computes the features in training and inside the saved
model, and the feature list is stored in the model file; a model refuses to predict if the
feature code has changed since it was trained.

\subsection{Target transform}
The terminal currents span more than ten decades, from OFF-state gate currents far below
\IFloor{} to ON-state drain currents in the microampere range, and $I_D$, $I_S$ change sign with
$V_D-V_S$. Regressing amperes with a squared loss would fit only the largest currents, so each
model predicts (\TargetKind{} transform)
\begin{equation}
  z_t = \operatorname{asinh}\!\left(\frac{I_t}{i_{0,t}}\right),\qquad
  t \in \{D, G, S, B\},
\end{equation}
where $i_{0,t}$ is the \AsinhQuantile{} quantile of $|I_t|$ on the training rows. For
$|I|\gg i_0$, $\operatorname{asinh}(I/i_0)\approx \operatorname{sign}(I)\ln(2|I|/i_0)$, so an
error $\delta z$ is a relative current error of about $\delta z$, which is exactly what we are
judged on. Unlike $\log_{10}|I|$, asinh is smooth through zero and carries the sign, so no
separate sign model is needed. A per-target $i_0$ is necessary because the currents live on very
different scales: one fixed $i_0$ cannot be ``small'' for all of them.

\paragraph{Channel shaping (ON cases).}
In the ON cases the channel current passes through zero at $V_D=V_S$, with the shape
$1-e^{-V_{DS}/v_T}$ in subthreshold and $\propto V_{DS}$ in the linear region. For
\ShapedCases, $I_D$ and $I_S$ are therefore divided by
\begin{equation}
  s(V_{DS}) = \tanh\!\left(\frac{V_D - V_S}{2 v_T}\right)
\end{equation}
(magnitude floored to avoid division by zero) before the transform and multiplied back
afterwards. The model then learns a smooth, one-signed quantity instead of a sign flip at
$V_{DS}=0$, where \LowVdsFrac{} of the ON rows are concentrated by design
(Section~\ref{sec:oversampling}). Shaping is \emph{not} used in the OFF cases: there $I_D$ and
$I_S$ near $V_D=V_S$ are dominated by junction leakage, which does not change sign with $V_{DS}$,
and shaping made them worse on validation.

\paragraph{Source current from Kirchhoff's law.}
The four terminal currents sum to zero, and $I_D$ and $I_B$ are predicted much more accurately
than $I_S$ (in the OFF cases $I_S$ is tiny and changes sign). The source current can therefore
also be derived as
\begin{equation}
  I_S = -(I_D + I_G + I_B),
\end{equation}
which in addition makes every prediction satisfy Kirchhoff's law exactly, a property the gate
level relies on. Both variants are scored on the validation set for every hyperparameter setting
and the better one is kept per case. The derived $I_S$ won in \KclCases. For the chosen model
type it lowers the validation p95 error (mean over currents) from \KclDirectPninetyfiveNmosOff{}
to \KclDerivedPninetyfiveNmosOff{} for \texttt{nmos\_off}, from \KclDirectPninetyfivePmosOff{} to
\KclDerivedPninetyfivePmosOff{} for \texttt{pmos\_off}, from \KclDirectPninetyfiveNmosOn{} to
\KclDerivedPninetyfiveNmosOn{} for \texttt{nmos\_on} and from \KclDirectPninetyfivePmosOn{} to
\KclDerivedPninetyfivePmosOn{} for \texttt{pmos\_on}.

\subsection{Model types and hyperparameters}
Four model types are compared for every case (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}): polynomial ridge
regression as an interpretable baseline, a random forest, gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM, one
regressor per current) and a multilayer perceptron (which also standardises its targets). Each
predicts all four transformed currents, inputs are standardised, and every estimator is seeded
(\SeedTraining). Each model type has a small grid of \GridPoints{} points, searched on the
validation set together with the two $I_S$ variants; everything else is fixed at the values in
the table.

\paragraph{Linear leaves in the GBDT.}
With constant leaves a boosted ensemble approximates the smooth log-current surface by a fine
staircase, which leaves a floor of a few per cent in the median error even with many trees.
LightGBM's linear trees fit a small ridge-regularised linear model in every leaf instead.
Since the log-current is close to linear in the physics features, each leaf then follows the
local slope and the staircase disappears.

\subsection{Metrics and selection}
Errors are relative, $e = (I_{ML}-I_{SPICE})/|I_{SPICE}|$. We report the median and 95th
percentile of $|e|$, the mean accuracy $100\,(1-\overline{|e|})$, and $R^2$ on $\log_{10}|I|$.
Rows with $|I_{SPICE}| <$ \IFloor{} are excluded from the relative errors: there the relative
error is dominated by numerical noise, while such currents contribute nothing to gate leakage.
This removes every OFF-state gate current. For every case the model with the lowest validation
95th-percentile error, averaged over the currents that have one, is selected (ties: median
error, then training time). We select on the p95 rather than the median because gate leakage is
a sum over several transistors, so the tail of the transistor error, not its typical value,
limits gate accuracy.

\paragraph{Why GBDT won.}
GBDT was the best model type in every case, by a wide margin on both the median and the p95
(Table~\ref{tab:models}). Polynomial ridge is far behind everywhere: a degree-2 or 3 polynomial
cannot follow a log-current that switches between exponential (subthreshold) and polynomial
(above-threshold) behaviour and has sharp structure at $V_{DS}\approx 0$ and at the pinned rails.
The random forest averages constant leaves without boosting, so it inherits the staircase error
at full size and cannot refine it; its median error is several times that of the GBDT. The MLP
is the strongest of the others in the OFF cases, where the targets are smooth, but it fits the
narrow, densely oversampled regions (low $|V_{DS}|$, pinned source and drain) less well than
trees, which split on exactly those coordinates. The GBDT combines both strengths: axis-aligned
splits isolate the special operating points, and linear leaves follow the smooth physics inside
each region.
% Re-check the qualitative ranking above (MLP second in OFF cases; "several times") after the
% final run: it is read from tab_models, not generated.

The OFF models are the most accurate; $I_B$ is the easiest current in every case. The
largest errors are the ON-state $I_D$ and $I_S$ p95 (Table~\ref{tab:chosen}), which come almost
entirely from rows near $V_D = V_S$, where the channel current crosses zero and a small absolute
error is a large relative one. Inside a gate these currents are not used directly: the channel
current of an ON transistor at $V_{DS}\approx0$ is fixed by the rest of the gate, and the ON
currents that do enter the gate leakage, $I_G$ and $I_B$, are predicted well. Section~\ref{sec:error}
quantifies this.
